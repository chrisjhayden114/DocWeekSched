# Stop chasing conference speakers

*By Chris Hayden, EdD, founder of Readyhall*

Every conference organizer I know has a version of the same ritual. Three weeks before the event, you open a spreadsheet with forty presenter names on it and start writing emails. *Just checking in on your slides.* *Gentle reminder about your bio and headshot.* *The program goes to print Friday. Any word?* By the final week you're sending them at midnight, and the last deck still arrives on a USB stick at the registration desk.

Nobody planned for the chase. It just happens, every time, to everyone, including me. I'm a researcher and educator who has organized conferences, PD days, and events for years, and the chase was the part I dreaded most. Not because presenters are unreliable (they're mostly generous volunteers with real jobs) but because the tools we hand them assume things that aren't true.

## Why the chase happens

Look at how presenter materials are usually collected and the problem explains itself.

**The tools assume presenters will make accounts.** Most event platforms ask speakers to register, verify an email, set a password, and log in to a portal they will use exactly once. A volunteer presenting one workshop at one Saturday conference has no reason to do any of that promptly, so they don't. Some platforms require speaker logins so firmly that their help centers have dedicated articles on speaker password resets. That's not a support article. That's a confession.

**The reminders are a human being.** No system knows the deadline, so a person (you) becomes the reminder system. Which means reminders happen when you remember, in whatever tone your exhaustion allows, and stop the moment you get busy with the two hundred other things an event requires.

**Nobody can see the whole picture.** Materials arrive by email, into a folder, occasionally by USB stick. The answer to "who's actually ready?" lives in your inbox's search bar. So you find out who wasn't ready at the worst possible moment: when the session starts.

## What calm collection looks like

When I built [Readyhall](https://readyhall.com), Speaker Readiness was the feature I designed first, around three decisions.

**1. No accounts. A personal link.** Every presenter gets their own link. Click it, see exactly what's needed (deck, bio, headshot, whatever you've defined), and upload. No registration, no password, no app. The link works for thirty days and can be re-sent or revoked at any time. When the barrier is one click, most people just do the thing.

**2. Reminders on a schedule, not a mood.** Readyhall reminds each presenter at seven days before their deadline, two days before, and once (only once) when overdue. The reminders are polite, specific, and stop the moment the person submits. You never write a checking-in email again, and no one gets nagged after they've done the work.

**3. One board with the truth on it.** Every presenter, every requirement, every status (submitted, in progress, overdue, approved) on a single screen. You review a submission and approve or reject it with one click, and rejections say why, kindly. The question "are we ready?" gets answered by a glance instead of an archaeology dig.

## What changes

The chase doesn't shrink. It disappears. The system does the remembering, the presenters do the uploading, and you do the one thing that actually needs your judgment: looking at what came in and saying yes. The strangest part, in my own events, was the silence. The week before, when the inbox would normally be on fire, was just quiet. Everything was on the board, and the board was mostly green.

There's a second-order effect, too. Presenters notice. Being asked for materials through a clean link with respectful reminders, instead of a chain of increasingly desperate emails, tells your speakers the event is run with care. For conferences that depend on volunteers coming back every year, that impression compounds.
